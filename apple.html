Apples

  1. There are over 7,500 varieties of apples.

    From sweet Honeycrisp to tart Granny Smith, apples come in thousands of varieties grown around the world.

  2. Apples are part of the rose family.

    They belong to the Rosaceae family, making them relatives of roses, pears, peaches, cherries, and strawberries.

  3. They float in water.

    About 25% of an apple's volume is air, which is why apples bob to the surface instead of sinking.

  4. An apple tree can live for over 100 years.

    While commercial orchards replace trees sooner for better yields, healthy apple trees can continue producing fruit for a century or more.

  5. Most of the nutrients are in the peel.

    The skin contains much of the apple's fiber, antioxidants, and vitamins, so eating the peel provides the greatest nutritional benefit.

  6. It takes years for a tree to produce fruit.

    Apple trees usually begin producing apples 3–5 years after planting, depending on the variety and rootstock.

  7. Apples naturally come in many colors.

    Besides red and green, apples can be yellow, pink, orange, or even deep purple, with some varieties having striped or speckled skins.

  8. Apple seeds contain a natural toxin.

    The seeds contain amygdalin, which can release tiny amounts of cyanide when crushed and digested. Accidentally swallowing a few whole seeds is generally not dangerous because they usually pass through the body intact.

  9. Apples stay fresh thanks to cold storage.

    Modern storage techniques can keep apples crisp for 6–12 months, which is why they're available almost year-round.

  10. The world's heaviest apple weighed over 1.8 kg (4 lb).

    A giant apple grown in Japan weighed 1.849 kg (4 lb 1 oz), earning a world record.

Bonus fact: Freshly cut apples turn brown because an enzyme called polyphenol oxidase reacts with oxygen in the air. A little lemon juice slows this reaction by lowering the pH and providing vitamin C, which acts as an antioxidant.
