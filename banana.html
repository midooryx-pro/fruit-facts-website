Bananas

  1. Bananas are technically berries.

    Botanically, bananas are classified as berries because they develop from a single flower with one ovary. Surprisingly, strawberries are not true berries.

  2. They are slightly radioactive.

    Bananas contain potassium, and a tiny fraction of that potassium is the naturally radioactive isotope potassium-40. The amount is so small that it's completely harmless.

  3. Banana plants aren't actually trees.

    What looks like a trunk is a tightly packed bundle of leaf bases called a pseudostem. Bananas grow on one of the world's largest herbaceous (non-woody) plants.

  4. One plant fruits only once.

    After producing a bunch of bananas, the main stem dies back, and a new shoot (called a sucker) grows to replace it.

  5. There are over 1,000 varieties of bananas.

    While the Cavendish banana is the one most people eat, there are hundreds of edible varieties, including red bananas, apple bananas, and tiny Lady Finger bananas.

  6. They're one of the world's most popular fruits.

    Bananas are among the most consumed fruits globally and are a staple food for millions of people.

  7. Bananas float in water.

    Like apples and watermelons, bananas are less dense than water, so they'll float.

  8. Ripeness changes nutrition.

    Green bananas contain more resistant starch, which acts like dietary fiber. As they ripen, that starch converts into natural sugars, making ripe bananas sweeter and easier to digest.

  9. The inside of a banana peel has practical uses.

    The soft inner peel has been used traditionally to help polish leather, soothe minor skin irritation, and add nutrients to compost, although scientific evidence for some of these uses is limited.

  10. The world's favorite banana has a disease problem.

    The Cavendish variety is threatened by a fungal disease called Tropical Race 4 (TR4), which has spread to many banana-growing regions and is driving efforts to develop more resistant banana varieties.

Bonus fact: Bananas naturally curve upward because they grow toward sunlight. This response to light is called negative geotropism (or gravitropism), where the fruit bends against the pull of gravity as it develops.
